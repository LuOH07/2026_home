Genome editing is a cornerstone of synthetic biology.
With the capacity to precisely knock out, insert, or replace specific DNA sequences, CRISPR has substantially broadened the scope of synthetic biology:
1. Enhancing target metabolite yields in model microbes;
2. Precisely engineering genomes to construct specialized chassis cells;
3. Developing biosensors responsive to environmental signals.
However, conventional CRISPR-Cas9 and Cas12 systems face notable limitations:
1. High plasmid burden: The large molecular size of Cas9/Cas12 effector proteins severely limits transmembrane transformation efficiency [1].
2. High heterologous expression toxicity: Abundant intracellular expression frequently causes non-specific cytotoxicity and imposes heavy metabolic burdens on host cells [2].
3. Low editing efficiency in non-model hosts: Most characterized promoters and sgRNA scaffolds are tailored to standard model strains, often leading to transcriptional incompatibility and restricted target recognition in non-model bacteria [3].
In this context, TnpB—a compact nuclease and evolutionary ancestor of Cas12,offers a promising path toward highly flexible, miniature editing tools.
Transposon-encoded TnpB nucleases provide a compelling template for compact genome editor development owing to their minimal molecular size [4].
While retaining RNA-guided endonuclease activity, the compact architecture of TnpB substantially reduces protein folding and expression bottlenecks, resulting in lower metabolic burden and superior cellular expression stability [5].
We selected the ISHahl1 subtype of TnpB from Halorhodospira halochloris, which exhibits high cleavage activity and well-defined native TAM (Transposon-Associated Motif; 5'-CTAC-3') recognition [6].
However, ISHahl1 TnpB remains constrained by its strict sequence preference for target-flanking motifs, hindering comprehensive target coverage across critical genomic regions.
Solution: STING
Using a "structure prediction–semi-rational design–experimental validation" workflow, we expanded the TAM recognition spectrum of ISHahl1 TnpB, successfully establishing an efficient, miniature genome editing platform.
We modeled ISHahl1 TnpB using AlphaFold3 and ESMFold, aligning its 3D coordinates with the ISDra2 ternary complex (PDB: 8EXA) to identify residues within the WED domain that directly interact with the TAM.
We performed targeted random mutagenesis on the predicted contact regions and cloned the resulting library into the pSTING backbone. Variants were subjected to positive and negative dual-selection using fluorescent and toxic reporter systems, followed by sequencing to identify potential relaxed-specificity variants [7]. Broadened TAM recognition was subsequently confirmed via plasmid interference assays.
Quantitative editing efficiencies derived from plasmid interference assays were used to train predictive machine-learning models for downstream iterative engineering.
Through semi-rational engineering, we expanded the targeting scope of ISHahl1 TnpB, delivering a versatile, high-efficiency genome editing solution.
